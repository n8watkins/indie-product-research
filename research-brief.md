# Reusable research brief: independent products that people use and pay for

Prepared for Nathan Watkins · November 6, 2026 (Los Angeles)

## Why improve the original prompt?

The original request has the right ambition but risks selecting winners, treating their retrospective explanations as causal proof, mixing consumer apps with selling advice to founders, and confusing publicity with customer satisfaction. “More successful” needs a metric: revenue, profit, longevity, audience, independence, or personal fit. A famous founder's current distribution is not a beginner's starting point. A better brief requires dated evidence, negative cases, comparable financial units, uncertainty, and falsifiable next actions.

## Copy and reuse this prompt

Act as an evidence-led product and distribution researcher. Study [ANCHOR FOUNDER] and five deliberately chosen independent founders to help me turn [PROJECTS / REPOSITORIES / LIVE URLS] into products used and paid for by [AUDIENCE]. My constraints are [HOURS/WEEK], [BUDGET], [CURRENT USERS], [REVENUE], and [EXISTING DISTRIBUTION]. Research as of [DATE].

First critique this brief and define success. Inventory the visible endorsers on [SEED PAGE], distinguishing direct book reviews from general praise, founders from media, and established outcomes from unverified claims. Select five comparators with useful differences; explain exclusions. Do not assume endorsement proves that a book caused business success. Add an outside comparator when it tests the anchor's advice.

Separate consumer, prosumer, B2B, marketplace, education, and sponsorship businesses. Distinguish solo founder, solo developer, contractors, and current team size. For money claims record date, currency, period, gross/net, recurring/one-off, product/portfolio, trailing revenue/run rate/lifetime sales, and whether independently verified. Exclude investment gains and valuation from app revenue comparisons. Never annualize a launch spike as a durable run rate.

Study the anchor's product history: original problem, buyer, promise, first useful outcome, initial distribution, ongoing acquisition, monetization, retention, costs, infrastructure, support, and failed attempts. Identify what existed before the large audience. Evaluate public customer reviews by product/domain/date/sample size/source incentives. Report both praise and complaints without treating selected reviews as a representative survey. Do not purchase products or bypass paywalls; disclose what cannot be inspected.

Develop a weighted 0–5 evidence rubric BEFORE assigning final comparison scores. Include problem urgency, reachable audience, time to value, willingness to pay, repeat use or economically valid episodic use, distribution, contribution margin, differentiation, and operational trust. Mark unknowns rather than scoring missing evidence as failure. Scores are research judgments, not validated predictions. Change criteria only when a counterexample reveals an omitted mechanism; do not adjust weights just to make a richer founder win.

Inspect my actual project documentation and customer journeys. Separate observed implementation from unverified deployment and traction. Explain where I lack evidence, not merely where I lack features. Recommend one primary commercial experiment and, at most, one small secondary experiment. For each: audience, trigger, positioning, demo, pricing hypothesis, acquisition channel, activation event, retention/occasion-return metric, costs, budget cap, stop/continue rule, and 14/30/90-day actions. Address SEO, paid acquisition, creators, communities, referral loops, brand naming, and whether the product should remain a useful free tool. Do not prescribe a rebrand or rewrite without evidence.

Evaluate which publicly accessible principles from [BOOK] still hold and which need updating for AI-assisted development. Label the limits of an assessment based on public material. Focus the report on businesses, not a book summary.

Deliver an executive decision memo, detailed report, full source register, claim ledger, scoring rationale, reusable prompt, project experiment backlog, and unanswered questions. Every consequential factual claim needs a nearby source. Label facts, founder claims, inference, and proposed tests. Prefer primary sources, check competing explanations, and include at least two failure cases. Preserve source dates and access date. Do not reproduce copyrighted source text or the paid book.

Save everything in a private GitHub repository [NAME], with a simple searchable, printable HTML report deployed to [HOST]. Delegate the presentation work; prioritize research. Verify the repository is private and report the deployed site's separate access setting. Do not imply a private repository makes its website private. Keep secrets and unrelated personal information out of the deployment.

## Report outline

1. Executive decision and immediate priorities.
2. Method, source quality, and what success means.
3. Levels's timeline, money claims, and product portfolio.
4. Mechanisms: market selection, distribution, delivery, monetization, operations.
5. Customer reviews and commercial failures.
6. Five founder cases and comparative evidence rubric.
7. MAKE: durable principles and necessary updates.
8. Solara, Open Ping, and CutelyAskMe: evidence-based project audits.
9. SEO, ads, naming, positioning, pricing, and practical economics.
10. Experiments, measurement, workload allocation, and 14/30/90-day gates.
11. Source register, limitations, and follow-up questions.

## Scope choices for this edition

Use public sources and repository evidence, not private analytics. Treat the report as a decision aid, not a forecast of Nathan's earnings. Use five contrasting comparators rather than five near-identical social-media personalities. Do not rank total wealth. Deployment is authorized; paid ads, contacting prospects, changing existing product code, and purchasing services are not part of this task.
